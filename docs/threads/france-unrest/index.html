France unrest

A cost-of-living and schools protest movement turned into France's worst unrest in years on 17 October. Macron blamed far-left groups and criminals, and the government is now asking towns to impose partial curfews; similar clashes have broken out in Milan.

DevelopingHoldingas of 19 Oct 2026

  1. Monday 19 October 2026
    DevelopingHolding

    France turns to curfews as cost-of-living clashes reach Milan

    Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu says he has asked some towns to impose partial curfews, after Macron promised that looters would pay.

    In his address on Sunday evening Macron blamed far-left groups joined by criminals, told prefects to work with mayors to protect public buildings and shops, including banning gatherings where needed, and said the prime minister would meet parties, unions and student groups this week. On Monday Lecornu said he had asked some municipalities for partial curfews, without naming them. Arrests from Saturday now stand near 2,400, and the Interior Ministry says nearly 1,000 police were hurt. Sunday was calmer: police used tear gas on a few hundred people at Place de la République.

    The far right's Jordan Bardella called the speech cowardly; Jean-Luc Mélenchon said the government alone was responsible. In Milan, police have arrested more than 200 people after two days of cost-of-living clashes, and Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni called a security meeting.

    Why it matters. Curfews mark a shift from managing marches to policing neighborhoods, and the Milan clashes suggest fuel-driven anger is not only a French problem.

  2. Sunday 18 October 2026
    Major shiftIntensifying

    France counts the damage, and waits for Macron

    The toll from Saturday's national protest day roughly doubled overnight: at least 1,800 arrests and more than 500 police, gendarmes and firefighters hurt.

    • Update, 5:58pm CT Macron addressed the nation on Sunday evening, blaming far-left groups joined by criminals and saying looters will pay for the damage. He told prefects to work with mayors to protect public buildings and shops, including banning gatherings where needed, and asked parties and unions to condemn the violence. Arrests now total nearly 2,400, according to the Interior Ministry.

    Authorities put Saturday's turnout at about 550,000; organizers said 800,000. Most marchers were peaceful, but masked groups looted businesses in Paris, Marseille, Lyon, Bordeaux, Nice, Limoges and other cities. Limoges's 19th-century city hall was set on fire and its roof collapsed, a Marseille police station was damaged, and Lyon's tourist office burned. Paris police alone made 855 arrests.

    A man died after collapsing on the Paris march route; police say he suffered cardiac arrest, and prosecutors are investigating. In Saint-Étienne, a 17-year-old suffered an amputation. The government blames radical far-left groups for starting the violence and opportunistic looters for spreading it. The mayors of Paris, Marseille and Lyon have asked to meet the prime minister.

    Why it matters. Saturday was a planned national march. The open question is whether disorder now sustains itself without one. What Macron offers tonight — concessions, a crackdown or both — sets the direction.
