Japan launches MMX to bring back the first soil from a Mars moon

An H3 rocket sent the probe toward Mars on Monday afternoon. It is due to land twice on Phobos and return about 10 grams of soil to Earth in 2031.

The Signal · Monday 19 October 2026

Key facts

  • The H3 rocket lifted off from Tanegashima at 2:41pm Central on 19 October (4:41am on 20 October in Japan).
  • The probe separated about 70 minutes after launch, as planned, Kyodo reports.
  • MMX is due to reach Mars in August 2027, land twice on Phobos and return about 10 grams of material in 2031.
  • NASA supplied a spectrometer and a sampler and has invested about $87 million; it will receive at least 15% of the sample.

What happened

Japan's space agency, JAXA, launched its Martian Moons eXploration probe, MMX, on Monday. The tenth H3 rocket, flying in its most powerful configuration with four solid boosters, lifted off from the Tanegashima Space Center at 3:41pm Eastern (2:41pm Central), which was 4:41am on Tuesday in Japan.

The rocket's second stage made a final burn to send the probe out of Earth orbit, and the spacecraft separated about 70 minutes after liftoff, as planned, Kyodo News reports. Staff at the launch site applauded when separation was confirmed. Kyodo says the rocket gave the probe a speed of about 11.15 km a second, the fastest of any H3 launch so far.

The plan

MMX is due to reach Mars in August 2027 and spend about three years there. It will enter a looping orbit around Phobos, the larger of the planet's two moons, map it to choose landing sites, then touch down twice to collect about 10 grams of surface material. A small rover built by the French and German space agencies, IDEFIX, is due to be dropped onto the surface in late 2028 or early 2029. The probe will also fly past the smaller moon, Deimos, before leaving for Earth in 2030; the sample capsule is due to land in Australia in 2031.

It is an international effort. NASA supplied a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer and a pneumatic sampler, provides deep-space communications and has invested about $87 million; at least 15% of the sample will go to NASA's Johnson Space Center. ESA, the French agency CNES and the German agency DLR are also partners.

Why Phobos

Scientists still do not know where Mars's moons came from. Their near-circular orbits above the equator suggest they formed from debris after a giant impact on Mars, much as Earth's Moon is thought to have formed. Their lumpy shapes and their colour in telescope readings look more like captured asteroids. Comparing Phobos soil with Martian rocks and meteorites should settle the question, and could also show how water and organic material moved around the early solar system.

If MMX succeeds, it will be the first mission to bring back material from the Mars system. The only previous attempt, Russia's Phobos-Grunt, never left Earth orbit after its launch in 2011. MMX builds on JAXA's Hayabusa and Hayabusa2 missions, which returned samples from asteroids.

Why it matters

A clean launch is only the first step of a five-year round trip, but it puts Japan on course to return the first samples from the Mars system, possibly before any from Mars itself. The soil should answer a long-running question about how Mars's moons formed.
