France turns to curfews as cost-of-living clashes reach Milan

Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu says he has asked some towns to impose partial curfews, after Macron promised that looters would pay.

The Signal · Monday 19 October 2026 · Updated 6:05pm CT

Since this morning

  • Update, 6:05pm CT

    Curfews are now named: around Place de la République and Les Halles in Paris from 11pm to 6am, with gatherings banned citywide from 8pm, plus Lyon, Nice, Coulaines and Lille, some for minors only. Macron refused Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez's offer to resign, and Lecornu said a state of emergency could follow if needed. The yellow vests have called new protests for Saturday.

Key facts

  • Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu has asked some towns to impose partial curfews; he has not named them.
  • Arrests since Saturday stand near 2,400, and the Interior Ministry says nearly 1,000 police were hurt.
  • About 550,000 people marched on Saturday, according to the Interior Ministry.
  • In Milan, police have arrested more than 200 people after two days of cost-of-living clashes.

From classrooms to the streets

The movement began in September in high schools in the working-class northern suburbs of Paris, over teacher shortages and run-down buildings, and spread to Bordeaux, Rennes, Nantes, Lyon, Lille and Marseille. Students were joined by unions, public-sector workers and "yellow vest" supporters, and the protests merged with a public-sector strike against the government's budget plans, which include a €54 billion package of spending cuts and tax measures and a freeze on civil servants' pay.

On Saturday about 550,000 people marched nationwide, according to the Interior Ministry. Most were peaceful, but looting and arson hit several cities, and a fire largely destroyed the historic town hall in Limoges.

The government's answer

In a televised address on Sunday evening, President Emmanuel Macron blamed far-left groups joined by criminals and promised that those responsible would be punished. He told prefects to work with mayors to protect public buildings and shops, including by banning gatherings where needed, and said the prime minister would meet parties, unions and student groups this week.

On Monday Lecornu said he had asked some municipalities to impose partial curfews. Arrests since Saturday now stand near 2,400, and the Interior Ministry says nearly 1,000 police were hurt. Sunday was calmer: police used tear gas on a few hundred people at Place de la République.

Reactions

The far right's Jordan Bardella called Macron's speech cowardly; Marine Le Pen called the protesters' demands legitimate but said the far left had taken them hostage. Jean-Luc Mélenchon said the government alone was responsible. Human Rights Watch says a 15-year-old lost a hand to a police stun grenade and a 14-year-old's jaw was broken by a tear gas canister fired at close range.

The unrest lands on a weak government: the 2024 election left no bloc with a majority, and the next presidential election is due in April 2027.

Beyond France

Cost-of-living anger is spreading. In Milan, police have arrested more than 200 people after two days of street clashes, and Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni called a security meeting. Watch which French towns impose curfews and whether nights stay calm, the prime minister's talks with unions and students, and any new national protest date.

Why it matters

Curfews mark a shift from managing marches to policing neighborhoods, and the Milan clashes suggest fuel-driven anger is not only a French problem.

What to watch

Which towns impose curfews, and whether nights stay calm; the prime minister's talks with unions and students; any new national protest date.
