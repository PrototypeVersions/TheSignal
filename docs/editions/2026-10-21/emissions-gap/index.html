UN says the world must now plan to overshoot 1.5°C and come back

The UN's annual emissions gap report, published on Wednesday, projects 2.6–2.8°C of warming on current pledges and policies and sets out the deep cuts needed to bring temperatures back to 1.5°C by 2100.

The Signal · Wednesday 21 October 2026

Key facts

  • Current policies point to 2.8°C of warming this century; fully delivered national pledges to 2.6°C; pledges plus net-zero targets to 2.1°C.
  • On the lowest path, warming peaks at 1.7–1.8°C around mid-century with about an even chance of returning to 1.5°C by 2100.
  • That requires cuts of 18% by 2030, 45% by 2035 and 69% by 2040 from 2025 levels.
  • Global emissions in 2025 stayed at a record level; the world has warmed by about 1.4°C.

The findings

The UN Environment Programme's Emissions Gap Report 2026, titled The Point of Return and released on Wednesday 21 October, finds that current policies would lead to about 2.8°C of warming this century. If countries delivered their national pledges in full, warming would reach about 2.6°C, and adding their net-zero targets would bring it to about 2.1°C.

Global emissions in 2025 stayed at a record level, statistically no different from 2024, with fossil carbon dioxide rising slightly, according to Channels TV's account of the report. The world has warmed by about 1.4°C. The largest national increase came from the United States, up 133 million tonnes, linked to higher power demand and more coal.

The way back

Rather than treating 1.5°C as still avoidable, the report sets out how to return to it. On the lowest possible overshoot path, warming peaks at 1.7–1.8°C around mid-century, with about a 50% chance of falling back to 1.5°C by 2100. That needs emissions cuts of 18% by 2030, 45% by 2035 and 69% by 2040 compared with 2025, far beyond the 5–8% by 2030 that full delivery of current pledges would bring. It would also require removing about 320 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide from the air over the century, roughly eight years of 2024 emissions.

There is some good news: since the Paris Agreement, renewable capacity has grown three times faster than expected and solar seven times faster, UNEP says.

The response

UN Secretary-General António Guterres said overshoot is not surrender, and UNEP's executive director, Inger Andersen, said emissions can be cut quickly. The report comes out each year ahead of the UN climate summit.

Why it matters

The UN's own yardstick now assumes the 1.5°C limit will be breached and measures the effort needed to come back down, which depends heavily on removing carbon on a scale that does not yet exist. The projections themselves barely moved from last year.
