FDA approves the first drug aimed at curing chronic hepatitis B

GSK's bepirovirsen, sold as Hibsago, let about one in five eligible patients in trials stop all treatment and keep the virus controlled, the first finite-course therapy approved for the infection.

The Signal · Wednesday 21 October 2026

Key facts

  • The FDA approved bepirovirsen on 20 October for adults without cirrhosis whose virus is already suppressed by antivirals.
  • In two phase 3 trials, 20% and 19% of patients reached a functional cure 24 weeks after stopping all treatment, against 0% on placebo.
  • Treatment is a weekly injection for 24 weeks alongside existing antivirals.
  • The label carries a boxed warning for serious liver reactions and low platelet counts.

The approval

The US Food and Drug Administration approved GSK's bepirovirsen, brand name Hibsago, on Tuesday 20 October for chronic hepatitis B, making it the first antisense drug approved for the infection. It is for adults without cirrhosis who have taken antiviral drugs for at least six months, whose virus levels are suppressed and whose level of hepatitis B surface antigen is at or below 3,000 IU/mL. The FDA also cleared a Roche blood test to select and monitor patients.

The drug works by targeting the virus's RNA, reducing the production of viral proteins including the surface antigen. Patients receive a weekly injection under the skin for up to 24 weeks while continuing their antivirals, and may then stop all treatment if they meet set criteria.

The evidence

Approval rests on two phase 3 trials, B-Well 1 and B-Well 2. A functional cure was defined as undetectable viral DNA and loss of surface antigen sustained for at least 24 weeks after all treatment stopped. At week 72, 20% of patients on bepirovirsen in B-Well 1 (127 of 650) and 19% in B-Well 2 (106 of 570) reached that point, against none on placebo. About 24% met the criteria to stop their antivirals.

Side effects were more common on the drug: severe adverse events occurred in 16% of patients during treatment against 3% on placebo, most often a rise in liver enzymes. The label carries a boxed warning for serious liver reactions and low platelets, which can raise the risk of bleeding, and the FDA advises regular blood tests.

Why it is different

Existing hepatitis B drugs suppress the virus but rarely clear it, so most patients take them for years. Wendy Carter of the FDA's drug centre said the new approach may let some patients keep the virus controlled after treatment ends. HCPLive notes that a functional cure does not necessarily mean the virus is eradicated, and that the effect has not been shown to last a lifetime.

Why it matters

For the first time, a regulator has approved a hepatitis B treatment designed to end, not just suppress, the infection. It works for a minority of carefully selected patients and carries real safety warnings, so it is a first step rather than a cure for all.
